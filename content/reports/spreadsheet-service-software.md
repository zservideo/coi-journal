## The three models

**Spreadsheet.** Someone on staff keeps a sheet of trades, policies, limits and expiration dates, and files certificates in a shared drive. Reminders are calendar entries or memory.

**Outsourced service.** A third party collects certificates from your trades, chases missing ones and reports status back. Some services also review against your requirements.

**Software.** A system stores certificates and requirements, sends renewal requests and flags problems. Some products read the documents and compare them with each project's requirements automatically. Others mainly store and remind.

Many companies end up with a mix: software plus a person, or a service that uses software.

## What each does well, and where it breaks

**Spreadsheets** are free, familiar and flexible. They break on three things: requirements that differ by project, endorsements that need reading rather than logging, and staff turnover. A spreadsheet only knows what someone typed into it.

**Services** add capacity. The question to ask is what "review" means. Collecting and logging a certificate is different from reading each endorsement against each project's requirements and telling you what fails. Ask for a sample deficiency report.

**Software** adds scale and reminders. The products differ most on depth: whether they check the endorsements themselves, handle different requirements per project, explain why something fails, and keep an audit trail you could show an owner or a carrier.

## A scoring method

Score each option from 0 to 3 on each line, weighted for your company.

1. **Requirements per project.** Can it hold different requirements per project and per trade?
2. **Endorsement review.** Does it read the endorsements, or only the certificate? See [additional insured](/dictionary/additional-insured/).
3. **Renewals.** Does it chase renewals before policies expire, and flag lapses? See [notice of cancellation](/dictionary/notice-of-cancellation/).
4. **Explanations.** When something fails, does it say what, why and what to ask for?
5. **Broker loop.** Can the trade's broker receive the deficiency and send the fix without an email chain?
6. **Audit trail.** Can you show who checked what, when?
7. **Fit.** Does it work with the tools you already run projects in?
8. **Cost.** Total cost per year, including your own staff time.

## On cost

Published cost estimates for manual tracking are scarce and mostly come from vendors. One, from BCS, puts it at $36,400 a year, based on 20 hours a week at $35 an hour. That is a self-reported estimate from a company that sells tracking software, and it measures only labor.

The bigger cost doesn't appear in any time sheet: the claim that lands on your policy because a gap went unnoticed. When you compare models, weigh how likely each is to catch the gaps that matter, not only the hours it saves. Our white paper [A certificate is not coverage](/reports/certificate-is-not-coverage/) lists the gaps to test for.

## A simple rule of thumb

- A few trades on a few jobs with uniform requirements: a disciplined spreadsheet can work, if someone reads the endorsements.
- Many trades, many jobs, different requirements per project: you need either a service that reviews endorsements or software that does, plus someone who owns the exceptions.
