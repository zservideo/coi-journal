## The law

Business Insurance describes Labor Law 240, often called the Scaffold Law, as a statute passed in 1885, with Labor Law 241 imposing safety protocols on the ground. Together they put liability for elevation-related injuries on owners and contractors in a way no other state does. The details of how courts apply them belong with counsel. What matters here is the effect on insurance.

## What it does to the market

The 2018 Business Insurance piece gathered numbers from brokers and carriers:

- **Cost.** Harry Johnson of Conner Strong & Buckelew compared a Pennsylvania project, insurable for roughly 3% to 3.5% of construction value, with New York, where he said he'd be lucky to get it done for 9%.
- **Deductibles.** He put a typical Pennsylvania deductible around $250,000, against about $2 million in New York.
- **Capacity.** Karen Reutter of Zurich said a half dozen or fewer insurers wrote New York City construction general liability.
- **Estimates.** A 2014 Rockefeller Institute report, often cited by opponents of the law, estimated related insurance costs at $785 million a year. Groups supporting construction workers criticized the study.

The debate hasn't gone away. In a March 2026 Commercial Observer opinion piece, Michael Coffey, an insurance litigation attorney, cited a Building Trades Employers' Association analysis putting New York premiums 200% to 500% above similar states. Those are advocacy figures, and we report them as such.

## What it does to certificates

A small, expensive market changes what trades buy and how it's written:

- **More surplus lines carriers.** No guaranty fund behind them. See [surplus lines carriers on certificates](/reports/surplus-lines-carriers-on-certificates/).
- **High deductibles and self-insured retentions.** A $2 million retention on a trade's policy means the first $2 million of a claim depends on the trade's balance sheet.
- **Narrower forms.** Labor law exclusions, action over exclusions, height limits and classification limits appear more often. A certificate never shows them. See [rented, falsified, excluded](/reports/rented-falsified-excluded/).
- **Additional insured wording that matters more.** If the GC's protection for a worker injury depends on the trade's policy, the AI endorsement, its primary and noncontributory wording and any contractual liability coverage carry the weight. See our briefs on [primary and noncontributory](/reports/primary-and-noncontributory-proof/) and [additional insured endorsements](/reports/additional-insured-endorsements/).

## A New York checklist

1. **Ask for the full general liability policy, or at least the exclusions schedule,** not just the AI endorsement.
2. **Look for labor law, action over, height and classification exclusions.** Any of them can remove coverage for the exact claim the law makes likely.
3. **Check the retention.** Know who pays before the insurer does.
4. **Check the carrier.** Rating, admitted or not.
5. **Check the umbrella.** Does it follow form on the exclusions, and does it pick up the AI?
6. **Use counsel and your broker** for the subcontract wording. This is the state where it matters most.
