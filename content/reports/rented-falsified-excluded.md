## Why these four

Every week, [Worth Reading](/worth-reading/) picks the construction insurance stories worth your time. This fall, four of them pointed the same way. Each one turned on something a certificate of insurance can't show by itself.

We read each one for a single question. If you were the general contractor, what would you have needed to see, beyond the certificate, to know the coverage was real? Our [white paper on the six ways certificates fail](/reports/certificate-is-not-coverage/) sets out the full list. This brief is the field evidence.

## Case 1: certificates for rent

**What was reported.** Florida authorities charged seven people in an alleged $100 million workers' comp scheme, [Insurance Journal reported](https://www.insurancejournal.com/magazines/mag-features/2026/09/07/883922.htm) in September. Prosecutors say shell companies bought thin coverage by underreporting payroll, then rented their certificates of insurance to subcontractors for a fee while crews were paid in cash. Ten insurers are named as alleged victims, and more than $2.4 million was seized.

**What the certificate could show.** A real policy, a real carrier and real policy numbers. In the scheme prosecutors describe, the certificate may well be genuine. The problem is that the policy behind it was sized for a payroll that didn't include the people on your site.

**The check that matters.** Make sure the named insured on the workers' comp certificate is the company that signed your subcontract and employs the crew. A certificate issued to a different company is a red flag, whatever it says about limits.

## Case 2: coverage from a company that couldn't write it

**What was reported.** A federal grand jury indicted two Destin, Florida men tied to The Wave Insurance and Hydrogen Deep, [according to Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southeast/2026/10/29/890770.htm). Neither company was licensed to write property and casualty coverage in Florida. Prosecutors allege the men collected more than $651,000 in premiums and issued falsified certificates of insurance. Both pleaded not guilty, and trial is set for December 14.

**What the certificate could show.** A carrier name. A certificate doesn't tell you whether that carrier is allowed to write the coverage in your state.

**The check that matters.** Look up the carrier with your state insurance department or the NAIC's [Consumer Insurance Search](https://content.naic.org/cis_consumer_information.htm) before you accept the certificate. It takes a few minutes, once per carrier, and you can reuse the answer across trades.

## Case 3: the exclusion that left nothing to pay

**What was reported.** An Illinois appellate court ruled that Mt. Hawley owes Stone Development no indemnity for a carpenter killed on his first day at a Waukegan hotel site, [Business Insurance reported](https://www.businessinsurance.com/insurer-prevails-in-coverage-dispute-over-carpenters-death/). The policy's employee injury exclusion applied, and Stone had no workers' comp in force. The estate holds a $2.65 million agreed judgment. Mt. Hawley paid $250,000 toward it.

**What the certificate could show.** A general liability policy with real limits. General liability usually excludes injuries to the insured's own employees, because workers' comp is meant to cover them.

**The check that matters.** Treat workers' comp as its own requirement, not a line to skim. If a trade's certificate shows general liability but no workers' comp, or workers' comp that has lapsed, stop and ask before the crew starts.

## Case 4: when trades sue each other

**What was reported.** In Bauer v. Scott Myers and Sons Roofing, the Idaho Supreme Court revived a plumber's negligence suit against a roofer after a balcony railing the roofer allegedly reinstalled gave way, [Business Insurance reported](https://www.businessinsurance.com/idaho-high-court-revives-injured-workers-lawsuit-against-contractor/). Whether one trade is shielded from suits by another trade's workers, the court said, turns on whether the general contractor controls the work, not on contract labels.

**What the certificate could show.** Limits and an additional insured box. It can't tell you whether the endorsements behind it will respond when one trade's worker sues another trade, and the GC is pulled in.

**The check that matters.** This is when real general liability limits, additional insured endorsements and waivers of subrogation get tested. Ask for the endorsements, not just the certificate. See [additional insured](/dictionary/additional-insured/) and [waiver of subrogation](/dictionary/waiver-of-subrogation/).

## The market backdrop

Carriers are leaning into construction. Zurich said its U.S. construction premiums grew 17% in the first nine months of 2026, while North American rates held flat, [Business Insurance reported](https://www.businessinsurance.com/zurich-north-american-premiums-rise-5-as-construction-business-grows/). When carriers compete for construction business on flat rates, brokers have more room to ask for the endorsements project requirements call for. It's a good time to ask.

## What the four cases have in common

None of these stories is about a sloppy certificate. Each turns on a question the certificate can't answer alone.

1. **Who does the policy actually cover?** The named insured, and whether it's the company on your site.
2. **Who wrote it?** A carrier licensed for that coverage in your state.
3. **What does it leave out?** Employee injuries under general liability, which is why workers' comp has to be in force.
4. **Will it respond when trades collide?** The endorsements themselves, not the boxes that summarize them.

All four questions can be answered before work starts. That's the practical lesson: the certificate is where verification begins, not where it ends.
