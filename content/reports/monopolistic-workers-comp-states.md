## Which states are monopolistic for workers comp?

In most states, employers buy workers comp from private insurers. In four, they can't. [IRMI](https://www.irmi.com/articles/expert-commentary/navigating-the-workers-compensation-residual-market) puts it plainly: in North Dakota, Ohio, Washington and Wyoming, all employers except those authorized to self-insure must buy workers comp from the state fund. [The Hartford's guide](https://www.thehartford.com/workers-compensation/monopolistic-states) adds that Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands also have state funds.

## Does state fund coverage include employer's liability?

A standard workers comp policy has two parts: Part One pays statutory benefits to injured workers, and Part Two, employer's liability, defends the employer against lawsuits related to those injuries. Most subcontracts require limits for both.

State fund coverage in monopolistic states doesn't include employer's liability, per The Hartford. Contractors there typically add it as **stop gap** coverage, often by endorsement to their general liability policy.

## Why does stop gap coverage matter to a GC?

Employer's liability is what responds when an injured worker's claim turns into a third-party suit, including claims that come back to the GC through indemnity. If a trade working in Ohio or Washington shows you only a state fund certificate, there may be no employer's liability coverage at all.

## What does workers comp proof look like in a monopolistic state?

- **No private workers comp policy on the [ACORD 25](/dictionary/certificate-of-insurance/).** The trade's state fund coverage is proved by the fund's own certificate or lookup, not the ACORD form.
- **Stop gap on the general liability policy.** Look for an employer's liability or stop gap endorsement with limits that match your requirement.
- **Multi-state trades.** A trade based in Indiana working on your Ohio job needs Ohio state fund coverage for that work. Its Indiana policy's other states coverage may not reach a monopolistic state.

Table: Workers comp proof in monopolistic states and everywhere else
| Item | Most states | Ohio, North Dakota, Washington, Wyoming |
|---|---|---|
| Who writes workers comp | Private insurers | The state fund, unless the employer is authorized to self-insure |
| Employer's liability | Part Two of the workers comp policy | Not in state fund coverage; bought as stop gap |
| Where the proof is | The ACORD 25 | The fund's own certificate or lookup, plus a stop gap endorsement |
| NCCI waiver of subrogation | Available | Doesn't apply; the fund sets its own rules |

## Can a trade give a workers comp waiver of subrogation in a monopolistic state?

Many subcontracts require a workers comp waiver of subrogation. The NCCI form most GCs know doesn't apply to state funds, which set their own rules. Check what the fund allows before you require it, and don't hold up a trade over a form that doesn't exist in that state. See our brief on [waivers of subrogation](/reports/waiver-of-subrogation-endorsements/).

## What employer's liability does FAR require in monopolistic states?

[FAR 28.307-2](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-28.3) requires $100,000 of employer's liability on covered federal contracts, except in states with exclusive or monopolistic funds. See our brief on [federal jobs](/reports/federal-contracts-far-insurance/).

## What should a GC check for trades in monopolistic states?

1. Know which projects sit in a monopolistic state.
2. Write requirements for those projects that ask for state fund proof plus stop gap, not a private workers comp policy.
3. Verify state fund coverage at the fund itself. See our brief on [workers comp lookups](/reports/workers-comp-coverage-lookups/).
4. Collect the stop gap endorsement and check its limits.
5. Diary both renewals.
